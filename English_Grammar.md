# Parts of sentence

- In the English language, a complete sentence represents a complete thought and must follow a strict structural order. Unlike some other languages, English sentences cannot have their parts rearranged at will.

- The foundational formula for an English sentence is: **Subject + Predicate**

## The Subject

- What it is: Who or what the sentence is about.

- Grammar role: It is always a noun (a person, place, or thing) or a pronoun.

- Example: In "The dog runs down the street," The dog is the subject.

## The Predicate

- What it is: What the subject is doing or what is happening to it.

- Grammar role: It starts with a verb (action) and usually contains an object (a noun receiving the action).

- Example: In "The dog runs down the street," runs down the street is the predicate (runs = verb; street = object).

## Object

## Core Formula

- **Subject + Verb + Object**

## Building Complexity

- Many English learners struggle because they try to memorize long, complicated sentences right away. The correct approach is to start with the most basic structure and layer information on top of it.

- By adding different parts of speech, you can smoothly transition a sentence from simple to advanced:

  - Basic: The dog eats food.

  - Adding Adjectives & Adverbs: The `big` dog eats food `quickly`.

  - Adding Prepositions: The big dog eats food quickly `under the bed`.

  - Adding Conjunctions: This big dog eats food `and` drinks water quickly under that bed.

# Word types

## Nouns

### What is a Noun?

- A noun is a word used to name something, including people, places, animals, physical objects, feelings, ideas, and events.

| Category        | Examples                         |
| --------------- | -------------------------------- |
| People          | teacher, bus driver, woman, boy  |
| Places          | New York, America, school, space |
| Animals         | dog, bird, cat                   |
| Physical things | pencil, apple, desk              |
| Feelings        | love, anger, sadness             |
| Ideas           | idea, information                |
| Events          | meeting                          |


### Singular and Plural Nouns

- Countable nouns often have singular and plural forms.
  - Singular: one apple.
  - Plural: two apples.

- Three common plural spelling patterns:

| Rule                                 | Singular | Plural   |
| ------------------------------------ | -------- | -------- |
| Usually add `s`                      | apple    | apples   |
| Words ending in `x`: add `es`        | fox      | foxes    |
| Consonant + `y`: change `y` to `ies` | cherry   | cherries |
| Vowel + `y`: keep `y`, add `s`       | toy      | toys     |

- Some nouns have irregular plural forms:
  - child → children
  - man → men
  - woman → women

- Not all nouns follow the simple singular/plural pattern.
  - For example: water, information, anger, sadness

### Common Nouns vs. Proper Nouns

- There are two main categories of nouns
  - Common noun: A general name for a person, place, or thing.
  - Proper noun: A specific name of a person, place, or thing.
    - Proper nouns normally begin with capital letters
    - A noun is not automatically proper just because there is only one. It must be used as a specific name.

| Common Nouns | Proper Nouns      |
| ------------ | ----------------- |
| man          | George Washington |
| woman        | Cleopatra         |
| city         | New York          |
| country      | America           |
| dog          | Specific dog name |

## Pronoun

### What is a Pronoun?

- A pronoun is a separate word type that helps us refer to people or things without repeating their names or nouns.
- The seven subject pronouns are: **I, You, He, She, It, We, They**

### Nouns and Pronouns Have Different Word Types

- Nouns and pronouns can perform similar grammatical functions, but they are not the same word type.
  - Some pronouns refer back to previously mentioned nouns. This is called reference.

- Example: The turtles ate carrots. They were happy.

| Word    | Word type          | Explanation              |
| ------- | ------------------ | ------------------------ |
| turtles | Common plural noun | Main noun in the subject |
| carrots | Common plural noun | Direct object            |
| They    | Plural pronoun     | Refers back to turtles   |
| happy   | Adjective          | Describes the turtles    |

- Example: A mountain has trees, rivers, and lakes.
  - mountain: common singular noun.
  - trees: common plural noun.
  - rivers: common plural noun.
  - lakes: common plural noun.

- Example: Queen Elizabeth sat on the bench.
  - Queen Elizabeth: proper noun, singular.
  - bench: common noun, singular.

- Example 3: He was full of anger and sadness.
  - He: singular pronoun.
  - anger: common noun, uncountable in this meaning.
  - sadness: common noun, uncountable in this meaning.

## Verb

### What is a Verb?

- Every predicate has a verb or a complete verb phrase at its center.
- A verb is a word that expresses:
  - Action
  - State
  - Happening
- Action: Something someone or something does.
  - The dog runs down the street.
- State: A condition or situation.
  - The dog is tired.
- Happening: Something that occurs.
  - It rained yesterday.

### The Traditional 12 English Tenses

- English grammar is commonly taught using 3 time areas and 4 patterns.

| Tense Pattern          | Present                    | Past                    | Future                    | Activity (When does the action happen?)                                                            |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Simple**             | Present Simple             | Past Simple             | Future Simple             | Habits, facts, or actions that happen at a specific time.                                          |
| **Continuous**         | Present Continuous         | Past Continuous         | Future Continuous         | Actions in progress at a specific point in time.                                                   |
| **Perfect**            | Present Perfect            | Past Perfect            | Future Perfect            | Actions completed before a specific reference point in time.                                       |
| **Perfect Continuous** | Present Perfect Continuous | Past Perfect Continuous | Future Perfect Continuous | Actions that started earlier and continued up to a specific reference point, emphasizing duration. |


| Tense Pattern          | Present             | Past               | Future                   |
| ---------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------ |
| **Simple**             | I talk              | I talked           | I will talk              |
| **Continuous**         | I am talking        | I was talking      | I will be talking        |
| **Perfect**            | I have talked       | I had talked       | I will have talked       |
| **Perfect Continuous** | I have been talking | I had been talking | I will have been talking |


### Four tense patterns

- Simple: Describes an action, fact, habit, or situation.
  - I work every day.
- Continuous: Describes an activity in progress around a particular time.
  - I am working.
- Perfect: Looks back from one point in time to something earlier.
  - I have finished my work.
- Perfect Continuous: Focuses on an activity continuing over a period of time.
  - I have been working for two hours.

### Regular Verbs vs. Irregular Verbs

| Verb Form                          | Meaning / Usage                                          | Example (Talk) |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| **Base Form (V1)**                 | Original form of the verb                                | Talk           |
| **Third-person Singular (V-s/es)** | Used with he, she, it in Present Simple                  | Talks          |
| **Simple Past (V2)**               | Used in Past Simple                                      | Talked         |
| **Past Participle (V3)**           | Used with have/has/had and in passive voice              | Talked         |
| **Present Participle (V-ing)**     | Used in Continuous tenses and as a gerund                | Talking        |
| **To-infinitive**                  | To + base form; used after certain verbs and expressions | To talk        |
| **Bare Infinitive**                | Base form without "to"; used after modal verbs           | Talk           |


#### Regular verbs

- Some forms follow the usual pattern
- Regular verbs generally form their Simple Past and Past Participle by adding **-ed** to the base form.
- Examples:
  - Talk → Talked → Talked
  - Work → Worked → Worked
  - Play → Played → Played

#### Irregular verbs

- Some forms don't follow the usual pattern
- Irregular verbs do not follow the regular **-ed** pattern when forming their Simple Past and Past Participle forms
- Examples

| Base Form | Simple Past | Past Participle |
| --------- | ----------- | --------------- |
| Eat       | Ate         | Eaten           |
| Go        | Went        | Gone            |
| Write     | Wrote       | Written         |
| See       | Saw         | Seen            |
| Cut       | Cut         | Cut             |
| Be        | Was/Were    | Been            |


### Subject-Verb Agreement

- The verb form can change depending on its subject.
- In the simple present:

| Subject | Verb  |
| ------- | ----- |
| I       | Talk  |
| You     | Talk  |
| We      | Talk  |
| They    | Talk  |
| He      | Talks |
| She     | Talks |
| It      | Talks |

- Remember: **He / She / It + V(s/es)**

### Tense vs. Mood

- Tense and mood are two different concepts.
- Tense: Shows when a situation happens and how it relates to time.
- Mood: Shows how the speaker presents the message.

- There are three common moods:

| Mood        | Purpose                                                | Example                      |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| Indicative  | used for ordinary statements and questions  | The dog is tired.            |
| Imperative  | used for commands or requests  | Please sit down.             |
| Subjunctive | appears in certain wishes,recommendations, demands, and unreal situations.| If I were you, I would wait. |

### Main Verbs, Helping Verbs and Modal Verbs

- A complete verb phrase can contain one or multiple words.


#### Main Verb

- The main verb carries the central meaning of the action, state, or happening.
- Example:
  - The dog runs down the street.
  - Here, runs is the main verb.

#### Helping Verb (Auxiliary Verb)

- A helping verb, also called an auxiliary verb, works with the main verb to form tenses, questions, and negatives
- Common auxiliary verbs:
  - Be: am, is, are, was, were, been, being
  - Have: have, has, had
  - Do: do, does, did
- Example:
  - She is standing on the table.
  - **Is** - Helping verb
  - **Standing** - Main verb

#### Modal Verb

- A modal verb is a type of helping verb. It expresses meanings such as:
  - Ability: can, could
  - Possibility: may, might, could
  - Permission: can, may
  - Necessity: must
  - Advice: should
- Example:
  - He could fly in the morning.
  - **Could** - Modal helping verb
  - **Fly** - Main verb

#### Practical Exercises

- Identify the complete verb phrase and its components.

- Exercise 1: The boy ate the grapes.
  - Complete verb: Ate
  - Main verb: Ate
  - Verb form: Irregular simple past of eat.
- Exercise 2: The bear could be in the cave.
  - Complete verb phrase: Could be
  - Modal helping verb: Could
  - Main verb: Be
- Exercise 3: She is dancing on the table.
  - Complete verb phrase: Is dancing
  - Helping verb: Is
  - Main verb: Dancing
